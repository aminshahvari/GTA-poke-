# Free, Invite-Only Online "Social Poker Club": Legal Framework in Ontario, Canada

*Prepared 2026-10-15. General information only. **Not legal advice.** Canadian gaming law is fact-specific, much of the case law is old, and no Canadian court has ruled on this exact model (a free online poker league with prizes funded by the organizer). Before you award prizes of real value, and especially before you open the club beyond friends, take any revenue, or send a player to a WSOP/WPT event, have an Ontario gaming or contest lawyer review the final design. Sections marked **[confirm with counsel]** flag the points that most need it.*

---

## 0. Bottom line

- **The core model is low risk:** free entry, no-cash-value play chips, leaderboard points, and prizes the organizer buys with his own money. Every relevant Criminal Code offence (common gaming house, betting, illegal lottery or "disposing of goods by a game of mixed chance and skill") depends on one of the following. Each is missing if the club is designed carefully.
  1. **Consideration** paid by the player. This means money, or "valuable consideration," to enter, play or win.
  2. **Stakes, a "bet" or a gain** flowing to the organizer, such as a rake, a fee or a bank.
- **Poker is a "game" in the Criminal Code sense.** It is a game of mixed chance and skill, and any chance at all is enough. So you cannot rely on "poker is skill." What keeps you safe is having **no consideration** and **no gain to the house**.
- **Licensing is not available for this app anyway.** Online, computer-operated lottery schemes can only be run by the province. In Ontario that means iGaming Ontario/OLG, with AGCO-registered operators. The charitable and social-gaming licences cannot cover games played "on or through a computer." The app therefore has to stay **completely outside** the gaming offences. It cannot be "licensed into" legality as a small operator.
- **The things that would turn it illegal or regulated:**
  - any entry, membership or subscription fee that unlocks play or prize eligibility
  - "donations" tied to play
  - a rake
  - selling chips or points, or letting them be bought
  - cashing points out, or letting members transfer or trade points or chips
  - paid features that affect entry, chips or results
  - wagering between members
- **Prizes:** Keep them organizer-funded and non-cash or cash-equivalent within reason. Points must be redeemable **only** for organizer-chosen prizes, never for cash, and must be non-transferable. Add a skill-testing question before awarding a prize. Without consideration it is a cheap extra safeguard, not a strict requirement.
- **Competition Act s. 74.06** (prize disclosure) technically applies only to contests that promote a product or a business interest. A purely private, non-commercial club among friends is probably outside it. Follow its disclosure rules anyway, because they are cheap and become mandatory once the club has any business angle (ads, sponsors, an app business).
- **Taxes:** For a recreational player, a prize from a game or contest is generally a non-taxable windfall in Canada. The organizer gets no deduction unless the club is part of a business.
- **Age:** The 19+ rule in Ontario attaches to regulated gaming, not to a free social game. Still, **require 19+**. It is the conservative choice and it matches Ontario gaming norms.

## 1. Criminal Code framework (Part VII, ss. 197–209)

### 1.1 Key definitions (s. 197(1))
Source: Criminal Code, R.S.C. 1985, c. C-46, Part VII, https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/c-46/page-33.html (fetched).

- **"game"** means "a game of chance or mixed chance and skill." Poker, including No-Limit Hold'em, is generally treated as a game of mixed chance and skill because the cards are dealt at random. McCarthy Tétrault says: *"Poker, for example, is considered to be a game of mixed chance and skill."* (Chambers Gaming Law 2025, Canada, https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/gaming-law-2025/canada.) The Ontario Court of Appeal has held that **"any degree of chance that is part of the game will suffice."** Canada has **no "dominant factor" test**, chance is judged by its **systematic** role, and the game is assessed from the perspective of the **average** player, not an expert. See *Play for Fun Studios Inc. v. Ontario (AGCO)*, 2019 ONCA 648, summarized at https://iagr.org/industry-news/legal-update-games-of-skill/. For the "systematic resort to chance" language, see also *Ross, Banks and Dyson v. The Queen*, [1968] S.C.R. 786 (cited in Osler, https://www.osler.com/en/insights/updates/when-games-become-gambles-criminal-law-implications-of-pay-to-earn-nft-games/). For the skill test applied to machines, see *R. v. Topechka*, [1960] S.C.R. 898.
  - *Research note:* I could not find, in sources that can be fetched, a reported Canadian decision that rules specifically on whether Texas Hold'em is a game of mixed chance and skill. Practitioners and regulators treat this as settled because of the *Ross* / *Play for Fun* "any chance" rule. **The analysis below assumes poker is a "game."**
- **"bet"** means a bet placed on any contingency or event. The Supreme Court in *R. v. Di Pietro*, [1986] 1 S.C.R. 250, is cited for two points. Gaming means playing a game for stakes, where participants wager money or property. Paying an entry fee to a contest is not itself a bet where the prize cannot exceed what was paid in. (See the secondary summary in Lexology In-Depth: Gambling Law – Canada (Segev LLP, 2025), https://segevllp.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Lexology-In-Depth-Gambling-Law-Canada.pdf, at paras 16, 28–29.)
- **"common gaming house"** means a place that is either:
  - (a) "kept for gain to which persons resort for the purpose of playing games", or
  - (b) kept or used for playing games:
    - (i) in which a bank is kept by one or more but not all of the players
    - (ii) in which any portion of the bets on, or proceeds from, a game is paid, directly or indirectly, to the keeper (this is the **rake**)
    - (iii) in which, directly or indirectly, **a fee is charged to or paid by the players for the privilege of playing** or using gaming equipment
    - (iv) in which the chances of winning are not equally favourable to all players, including the person, if any, who conducts the game
- **"keeper"** is defined broadly. It includes anyone who has "the care or management of a place." The organizer of an online club would be the "keeper."
- **s. 197(4):** A place can be a common gaming house even if only part of the game is played there, or the stake is somewhere else. Whether a website or server is a "place" has not been squarely decided for online poker. Assume that it can be. **[confirm with counsel]**

### 1.2 Offences
- **s. 201(1): keeping a common gaming house.** This can be prosecuted as an indictable offence (up to 2 years) or on summary conviction. **s. 201(2)** covers people "found in" one and owners who knowingly permit one.
- **s. 202(1): betting, pool-selling and bookmaking.** Examples are recording or registering bets, selling pools, "engaging in the business or occupation of betting," and (h) advertising "any offer, invitation or inducement to bet." These do not apply where nobody places a bet (https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/c-46/page-34.html).
- **s. 204(1)** exempts, among other things, "a private bet between individuals not engaged in any way in the business of betting." It also exempts stakeholders for bets among "not more than ten individuals." **Do not rely on it.** The club design should involve no bets at all.
- **s. 206(1): lotteries and games of chance.** Key paragraphs:
  - (a) making or advertising "any proposal, scheme or plan for advancing, lending, giving, selling or in any way disposing of any property by lots, cards, tickets or any mode of chance whatever"
  - (d) conducting "any scheme… for the purpose of determining who… are the winners of any property so proposed to be… given…"
  - (e) "swapping up" schemes, where a person, "on payment of any sum of money," can receive more because of other people's payments
  - (f) disposing of "any goods, wares or merchandise by any game of chance or any game of mixed chance and skill **in which the contestant or competitor pays money or other valuable consideration**"
- **s. 207: permitted lottery schemes.**
  - (1)(a) The province may conduct and manage lottery schemes.
  - (1)(b) Charities and religious organizations may run them under a provincial licence.
  - (1)(d) A licensee may run them at a public place of amusement, with prizes of $500 or less.
  - **s. 207(4)(c):** For paragraphs (1)(b) to (f), a lottery scheme **does not include** one "operated on or through a computer, video device, slot machine or a dice game." **Result: only the province can lawfully run online gaming.** No charitable, social or "fair" licence can authorize an online poker room (https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/c-46/page-35.html).
- **s. 209: cheating at play** with intent to defraud. This is relevant to integrity measures such as collusion and multi-accounting, but it does not depend on stakes.

### 1.3 Applying the elements: prize, chance, consideration (plus "gain")
Canadian contest and gaming lawyers boil the s. 206 lottery offences down to three elements: **prize + chance + consideration**. If any one is removed, the contest is generally lawful (Gowling WLG, https://gowlingwlg.com/en-ca/insights-resources/articles/2023/demystifying-contest-laws-in-canada; contestlawyer.ca, https://www.contestlawyer.ca/skill-testing-questions/).

| Element | Your app | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| **Prize** | Yes, once points are redeemable for gift cards or merchandise | Play chips alone, with no cash value, are not a prize. Points that can be redeemed for goods **are** a prize in "money's worth." |
| **Chance** | Yes. Poker is mixed chance and skill. | Cannot be removed. A season-long leaderboard adds more skill, but chance remains. |
| **Consideration** | **No.** Entry is free, and the organizer funds everything. | **This is the element that has to stay absent.** |
| **Gain to keeper / fee / rake / bank** (s. 197 common gaming house) | No | Must stay absent. |

- **s. 206(1)(f)** expressly requires that the competitor pays money or other valuable consideration. Without consideration, it does not apply.
- **s. 206(1)(a) and (d)** do not mention payment on their face. Courts and practitioners have nonetheless generally treated free, "no purchase necessary" contests as outside the lottery offences. McCarthy Tétrault puts it this way: games of chance and of mixed chance and skill are illegal lotteries **"unless no consideration is paid to enter, play or win a prize"**, and "hosting a competition with no entry payment is not illegal" common gaming or betting house activity, provided "no consideration is payable in order to enter the competition" (Chambers 2025). Osler, citing a 1982 Ontario Court of Appeal decision (39 O.R. (2d) 314), says the prohibitions do not apply where the user does not have to pay to play. Because (a) and (d) are drafted literally, Canadian sponsors still customarily add a **skill-testing question** as a second safeguard (see §4).
- **Common gaming house:**
  - Paragraph (a) requires the place to be "kept for **gain**." The definition does not say whose gain, or what kind. An organizer who earns nothing should not meet it. **If the club later earns money, for example from ads, sponsorships, premium subscriptions or affiliate links to real-money sites, there is an argument that it is "kept for gain."** That is a key reason to keep any monetization fully separate from play, or to avoid it. **[confirm with counsel before any monetization]**
  - Paragraph (b)(ii) (rake) and (b)(iii) (fee for the privilege of playing) are avoided if there is no rake and no fee of any kind, direct or indirect.
  - Paragraph (b)(i) (bank) and (b)(iv) (unequal chances): tournaments where the house does not play and every player has the same odds avoid these. Never let a house account or bots hold the bank or play with an edge. Any free-play "house" accounts should be disclosed and should not be eligible for prizes.
- **s. 197(2) social-club exception.** This protects only an *incorporated genuine social club*, and only where no part of the bets or proceeds goes to the keeper and no fee is charged for participating, except under a provincial licence. The burden of proving it is on the accused (s. 197(3)). It is narrow and does not fit an informal online friends' group. **The app's safety does not come from s. 197(2).** It comes from having no stakes, no fee and no gain, so that none of the definitions is met in the first place.

### 1.4 When do points become "money's worth"?
Play chips are not stakes when they cannot be bought, sold, transferred or cashed out, and have no cash value. Points become **money's worth** (and a "prize") once they can be exchanged for anything of value. That is acceptable **as long as no consideration goes in**. The model breaks when value can flow **into** the system (people buying chips or points, paying fees, "donating") or **out** of it in a fungible way (cashing out points, letting players trade or sell chips, points or accounts). That would create stakes or a secondary market. Osler's NFT analysis makes the same point: once in-game items "can be traded and sold for money or money's worth," and players pay to play, the lottery and gaming provisions are engaged (Osler, above). Keep points **non-transferable, non-purchasable, not redeemable for cash, and redeemable only for organizer-chosen prizes.**

## 2. What would make it illegal or require a licence

| Feature | Risk | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Entry fee or buy-in (even "small") | **Illegal** | Consideration → s. 206(1)(f); a fee for playing → common gaming house (b)(iii); real-money gaming requires AGCO/iGO |
| Club membership fee, dues or subscription that unlocks play or prize eligibility | **Illegal / high risk** | An indirect fee "for the privilege of playing" (s. 197(1)(b)(iii) says "directly or indirectly") |
| "Donations" tied to play, seats, re-buys or chips (even "voluntary but expected") | **High risk** | Treated as consideration in substance |
| Rake, or any cut of the pot, prize pool or proceeds | **Illegal** | Common gaming house (b)(ii) |
| Selling play chips, re-buys, add-ons or points | **Illegal** | Consideration plus stakes |
| Letting points or chips be bought, gifted for money, or traded between players | **High risk** | Creates value and stakes, and a secondary market |
| Cashing out points for money, or for open-loop or prepaid cards that act as cash | **High risk** | Turns chips or points into stakes. An occasional gift card as a *prize* from the organizer is fine. A cash-out *mechanism* is not. |
| Side bets or wagering between members (including off-platform, if organized through the club) | **Illegal / high risk** | Betting and gaming; s. 204's private-bet exemption is narrow |
| "Premium" features that affect entry, chips, seats, re-entries or results | **High risk** | Indirect consideration. Purely cosmetic features (avatars, themes) with no effect on play or prizes are lower risk, but still create the "kept for gain" issue. Best avoided. |
| Advertising to the public or open sign-up | **Moderate** | Not an offence by itself if free, but it raises visibility, brings in the Competition Act, and creates risk of being seen as a gaming site. s. 202(1)(h) bars advertising inducements to bet, so never use betting language. |
| Prizes of high value | **Moderate** | Not illegal without consideration, but they increase scrutiny and tax questions, and make sloppy features (fees, transfers) far more dangerous. Keep values modest and documented. |
| Requiring a purchase from the organizer, a sponsor or an affiliate to enter or redeem | **Illegal** | Consideration |
| Linking to, or affiliating with, real-money offshore poker sites | **High risk** | AGCO standards target unregistered gaming and its promoters (DLA Piper, https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/publications/2022/11/agco-aims-to-end-unregulated-ontario-igaming-market) |
| Rewarding recruitment with payments, or payment-based tiers | **High risk** | Pyramid / "swapping-up" (s. 206(1)(e)) if any payment is involved |

**There is no licence that fixes these problems online.** s. 207(4)(c) excludes computer-operated schemes from charitable and other licences. The only lawful real-money online poker in Ontario is through AGCO-registered operators under an operating agreement with iGaming Ontario. That costs about $100,000 per site to register plus large investigation and annual fees (Chambers 2025). It is not realistic for a friends' club.

## 3. Ontario specifics

### 3.1 AGCO / iGaming Ontario
- Ontario's regulated online market covers "lottery schemes conducted and managed by OLG or iGaming Ontario that are played or operated through the internet." Operators must be **AGCO-registered** and have an **operating agreement with iGO** (AGCO, Registrar's Standards for Internet Gaming, https://agco.ca/en/book/export/html/245361; DLA Piper, above).
- **Does a free, no-purchase social game with prizes need registration?** I found no basis for that in the sources reviewed. The registration framework applies to lottery schemes, meaning gaming for money or money's worth with consideration. McCarthy Tétrault: **"Social gaming with no prize in money or money's worth is not regulated per se by any government body"** (Chambers 2025). A free contest with prizes is analysed as a promotional contest under the Criminal Code and the Competition Act, not as iGaming. As long as there is no consideration and no rake or fee, the club is not "conducting and managing a lottery scheme" and should not need registration. **[confirm with counsel. The Gaming Control Act, 1992 text on e-Laws (https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/92g24) could not be fetched because it needs JavaScript, so I did not quote its definitions directly.]**
- The Registrar's Standards also require that free-to-play games *on regulated sites* have "the same odds of winning as games played for money." This is a reminder to make sure your RNG and dealing are fair and auditable.
- AGCO advertising guidance says affiliates must not "advertise gaming sites that operate in Ontario without AGCO registration," and that "free means free" (https://www.agco.ca/en/news/important-information-advertising-and-marketing-ontarios-new-igaming-market). Do not link to, promote or take referral money from real-money poker sites.

### 3.2 Charitable lottery and raffle licensing
- Ontario licenses charitable gaming through municipalities and the AGCO. Raffles with total prizes of **$50,000 or less** and bingo with **$5,500 or less** are licensed by the municipality. Larger events, and **electronic raffles**, are licensed by the AGCO (https://www.agco.ca/en/lottery-and-gaming/where-get-lottery-licence). Under s. 207(1)(b), these licences are only for **charitable or religious organizations** whose proceeds go to charitable purposes.
- **Not relevant to the club.** The club sells no tickets and is not a charity. Do **not** run raffles, "50/50s" or paid prize draws inside the club. They would need a charitable licence that the club could not get.

### 3.3 AGCO Social Gaming Licence
- This licence allows casual games in public places, such as euchre, cribbage and bridge. Bets are capped at **$2 per person**, cash prizes at **$500 per day**, no admission fee is allowed, and players must be 18+. Critically, games may **not** be played **"on or through an electronic device,"** and "casino games are not permitted" (https://www.agco.ca/en/lottery-and-gaming/social-gaming-licence-guide). **It does not cover an online poker app** and gives no route to small-stakes online play.

### 3.4 Ontario prize-promotion rules
- Ontario has **no statute specific to contests** like Québec's (and Québec is out of scope here). Prize promotions in Ontario are governed by the Criminal Code (s. 206), the federal Competition Act (s. 74.06 and the general misleading-advertising rules), and general consumer protection and privacy law.

## 4. Contest rules and the Competition Act

### 4.1 Section 74.06
Text (https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/c-34/section-74.06.html): it is reviewable conduct, "for the purpose of promoting, directly or indirectly, the supply or use of a product, or… any business interest," to conduct any contest or game of chance, skill, or mixed chance and skill, or otherwise dispose of any product or benefit by such a mode, where:
- (a) "adequate and fair disclosure is not made of the number and approximate value of the prizes, of the area or areas to which they relate and of any fact within the knowledge of the person that affects materially the chances of winning"
- (b) distribution of prizes is "unduly delayed"
- (c) "selection of participants or distribution of prizes is not made on the basis of skill or on a random basis in any area to which prizes have been allocated"

**Scope:** It applies only to contests run to promote a product or business interest. A private, non-commercial friends' club is probably outside it. A grown club with an app brand, ads, sponsors or plans to commercialize would likely be inside it. **Follow it from day one anyway.**

**Competition Bureau guidelines** (https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/promotional-contests-enforcement-guidelines; these are 2009 guidelines that the Bureau says are under review):
- Disclose "in a reasonably conspicuous manner **prior to the potential entrant being inconvenienced** in some way or committed."
- "Approximate value" means approximate regular market value.
- Disclose regional allocation, the total number of prizes, any early-bird dates, and anything that materially affects the chances of winning.
- If no purchase is required, say so prominently.

**Recommended disclosures in the app** (short "mini-rules" plus full rules):
- Who is eligible: 19+, Ontario/Canada residents, invite-only
- The prize catalogue: each prize's approximate retail value and the number available per season
- How points are earned (the points table), the season dates, and the redemption window
- That winners are decided by tournament results (mixed skill and chance), not random draw
- Tie-break rules
- The skill-testing question requirement
- That no purchase is necessary, there is no cash value, points are non-transferable and there is no cash alternative
- How and when prizes are delivered, which should be prompt
- How to contact the organizer about disputes
- Any regional limits, for example "Open to residents of Canada excluding Québec" (Québec has separate contest rules)
- The right to disqualify for cheating or collusion

### 4.2 Skill-testing question
- **Purpose:** It adds a skill element so that a chance-based promotion falls outside the s. 206 lottery offences. The customary form is a four-step, two-digit arithmetic question answered without help (Gowling; contestlawyer.ca).
- **Does it matter with no purchase?** Strictly, the free-entry route already removes consideration, which is the element that matters most. A poker leaderboard also already involves skill. contestlawyer.ca notes that conservative sponsors remove *two* of the three elements, and that once "consideration is… also removed, running a contest becomes low risk." Because s. 206(1)(a) and (d) do not mention payment on their face, and poker still involves chance, **including a skill-testing question costs nothing and is recommended** before a prize is released. It is a second layer of protection, not the main one.

## 5. Sending top players to WSOP / WPT

- **Canadian criminal side.** The organizer paying a buy-in, a seat package or travel as a **prize**, with no payment from entrants, is a free contest with a prize. It is legal on the same analysis as a gift card. Considerations:
  - **Value and disclosure.** A $10,000 Main Event seat plus travel is a high-value prize. Disclose it, describe exactly what is covered and what is not (buy-in, flights, hotel, per diem, taxes and fees), and set substitution rules ("organizer may substitute a prize of equal or greater value").
  - **Award the prize outright. Do not stake.** Award it as an unconditional prize. If the organizer takes a percentage of the player's winnings in return ("staking" or "backing"), the arrangement becomes a gambling investment or business deal. That could look like the organizer having a gain or stake in gaming, and it raises tax and contract issues. **Do not mix staking into club prizes.** If anyone wants a staking deal, it should be a separate written contract between adults. **[confirm with counsel]**
  - **Eligibility conditions.** Winners must be of legal gambling age at the venue (21+ in Nevada), hold a valid passport, and be able to enter the US. State these conditions up front, along with a fallback such as a runner-up or a cash-equivalent alternative. A cash alternative is acceptable as an organizer-funded prize. It is not a cash-out of points.
  - **Pay the vendor directly.** Preferably pay the buy-in or registration to the event or a travel provider, rather than giving cash to the player.
  - **Wind-down.** Make sure points do not accumulate into an expected entitlement. Keep a clause like "organizer may modify or end the prize program; no cash value."
- **Taxes in Canada.** The prize (the seat or trip) is generally a non-taxable windfall for a recreational player (§6). Any **winnings at the event** are, for a recreational Canadian player, generally not taxable in Canada either.
- **US side (out of scope, one line):** US tax withholding and reporting on winnings by non-residents, treaty claims, and US entry and visa matters should be checked with a cross-border tax adviser.

## 6. Taxes

- **Winners (individuals).** The CRA says lottery winnings and similar gifts or windfalls are generally not reported or taxed. The exception is a prize that is employment, business or property income, or a "prize for achievement" (CRA, "Amounts that are not reported or taxed," https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/individuals/topics/about-your-tax-return/tax-return/completing-a-tax-return/personal-income/amounts-that-taxed.html). Recreational gambling or contest winnings are generally non-taxable windfalls. Professional-gambler status, where winnings are business income, is rare. It is assessed on organization, skill, information, extent of activity and intention. See *Leblanc v. The Queen*, 2006 TCC 680, and the discussion at LawNow, https://www.lawnow.org/good-luck-taxable/. Any income *earned on* a prize, such as interest, is taxable. The detailed CRA guidance is Income Tax Folio S3-F9-C1, *Lottery Winnings, Miscellaneous Receipts, and Income (and Losses) from Crime* (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/technical-information/income-tax/income-tax-folios-index/series-3-property-investments-savings-plans/series-3-property-investments-savings-plans-folio-9-miscellaneous-payments-receipts/income-tax-folio-s3-f9-c1-lottery-winnings-miscellaneous-receipts-income-losses-crime.html). That page could not be fetched because of a URL-length limit, so I relied on the CRA summary page and secondary sources.
  - **Caveats:**
    - If a winner is the organizer's employee or contractor, or the prize is tied to work, the prize may be taxable employment or business income.
    - A player who earns a living from poker could be in a different position.
    - Prizes "for achievement in a field of endeavour ordinarily carried on by the taxpayer" can be taxable (CRA's former IT-213R position, per LawNow).
- **Organizer.**
  - If the club is a personal hobby, prizes are personal spending from after-tax money. They are not deductible, and no information slips are normally required for windfall prizes to unrelated individuals.
  - If the club becomes a business (ads, sponsors, an app company), prize costs may be deductible promotional expenses, and HST/GST and information-reporting questions arise. **[confirm with an accountant]**
  - Keep records of prize purchases, recipients and values.

## 7. Age

- Ontario's gambling age is **19**, for casinos, online gaming and sports betting (lottery scratch tickets are 18+) (LegalLine, https://www.legalline.ca/legal-answers/what-is-the-legal-age-to-gamble/). AGCO's iGaming standards make individuals under 19 ineligible to play on regulated gaming sites (Registrar's Standards, Std 3.01). The AGCO Social Gaming Licence uses 18+.
- **Does it legally apply to a free social game?** Not directly. These age rules attach to regulated gaming, and a free, no-consideration game is not regulated gaming.
- **Recommendation: require 19+.** This matches Ontario gaming norms and avoids encouraging minors to gamble. It also ensures winners can contract and receive prizes in their own name, makes WSOP/WPT prizes realistic (21+ in Nevada), and reduces reputational and privacy risk (children's data). Collect a date-of-birth attestation at invite acceptance, and verify ID before releasing any significant prize.

## 8. Safe-harbour design checklist

### DO
1. Keep entry, play, re-entries and prize eligibility **100% free**. No purchase, fee, subscription, donation or data-for-value requirement of any kind.
2. Give play chips **no cash value**. They cannot be bought, sold, transferred, gifted or cashed out, and they reset each tournament.
3. Keep leaderboard points **non-transferable and non-purchasable**, with **no cash value**, redeemable **only** for organizer-chosen prizes from a published catalogue.
4. Fund all prizes **solely from the organizer's own money** (or later from disclosed sponsors who receive no payment from players).
5. Publish **Club Rules / Prize Terms** before the season: prize list with approximate retail values and quantities, points table, season dates, tie-breaks, eligibility, how winners are decided, delivery timing, and the skill-testing question.
6. Require a correct **skill-testing question** before releasing any prize.
7. Keep the club **invite-only**, **19+**, **Canadian residents** (consider excluding Québec), with one account per person.
8. Use a **fair, certified-quality RNG**. The house never plays for prizes, every player has equal odds, and logs are kept for disputes.
9. Enforce anti-collusion, anti-multi-accounting and anti-chip-dumping rules, with a right to disqualify.
10. Deliver prizes **promptly**. Pay vendors directly for big prizes such as event seats and travel.
11. Keep records of prizes awarded, values and recipients.
12. Reserve the right to modify or end the prize program, with notice, without any cash alternative to points.

### NEVER
1. Never charge entry, buy-in, re-buy, add-on, membership, subscription or "premium" fees connected to playing or to prize eligibility.
2. Never take a rake or any cut, or let anyone "bank" games.
3. Never sell or allow buying of chips or points, and never let points or chips or accounts be transferred or traded.
4. Never offer cash-out of points, or a points-to-cash or crypto conversion.
5. Never allow or facilitate wagering between members, side pots for real money, or "settle up later" arrangements.
6. Never tie "donations" or tips to seats, chips or prizes.
7. Never advertise the club publicly as gambling or betting, and never link to, or affiliate with, real-money poker or casino sites.
8. Never run raffles, 50/50s or paid draws inside the club.
9. Never stake players (take a share of event winnings) as part of the prize program.
10. Never let minors play.
11. Never introduce ads, sponsorships, paid tiers or any revenue **without first getting legal advice**. Revenue can create "kept for gain" and Competition Act issues.

### Before growing the club
Get a lawyer's review before any of the following:
- going public or open sign-up
- allowing more than roughly a few dozen members outside the friend network
- adding any monetization
- offering high-value prizes (for example over about $1,000 per prize, or a WSOP/WPT package)
- opening to players outside Ontario

## 9. Suggested "Club Rules & Prize Terms" text (short form for the app)

> **[Club Name] — Club Rules & Prize Terms**
>
> 1. **Private, invite-only club.** Membership is by personal invitation from the Organizer only. Members must be **19 years of age or older** and residents of Canada (excluding Québec). One account per person.
> 2. **Free to play. No purchase necessary.** There are no entry fees, buy-ins, membership fees, subscriptions or donations of any kind to join, play, or be eligible for prizes. Nothing you buy or pay will improve your chances.

> 4. **No wagering.** Members may not bet or wager money or anything of value on any game or with each other through or in connection with the Club. The Club takes no rake, fee or share of anything.
> 5. **Leaderboard points.** Points are awarded by finishing position under the published points table. Points are **not property**, have **no cash value**, are **non-transferable**, cannot be purchased, and **cannot be exchanged for cash**.

The current prize catalogue, number of prizes and approximate retail values are listed in the app. Points may be redeemed only for catalogue prizes, subject to availability. > 10. **Changes.** The Organizer may change, suspend or end the points or prize program at any time with notice. Unredeemed points have no value and no cash alternative.
> 11. **Taxes and liability.** Winners are responsible for any taxes or costs not expressly included in a prize. Participation is at your own risk; the Club is a private social activity and not a gambling service.
